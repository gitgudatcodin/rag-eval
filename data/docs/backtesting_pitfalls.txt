Backtesting means simulating a trading strategy on historical data to estimate how it would have performed. Done well, it filters out bad ideas before they touch real capital. Done poorly, it manufactures false confidence.

The most dangerous pitfall is look-ahead bias: accidentally using information that was not available at the time of the simulated trade. A classic example is ranking stocks on financial ratios from annual reports before those reports were actually published. The fix is point-in-time data, where every input is timestamped by when it became known.

Survivorship bias is the second trap. If your historical universe only contains companies that exist today, you have silently deleted every bankruptcy and delisting, which flatters the results. A proper backtest reconstructs the investable universe as it looked on each historical date.

Overfitting is the third. With enough parameters, any strategy can be tuned to look brilliant in-sample. Defenses include keeping models simple, testing on out-of-sample periods, and demanding that performance hold across different market regimes and countries.

Finally, a backtest is not reality: it usually ignores transaction costs, market impact, and the fact that publishing a strategy can erode its edge. Treat backtest returns as an upper bound, not a promise.
