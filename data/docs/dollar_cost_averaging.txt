Dollar-cost averaging means investing a fixed amount of money on a regular schedule, regardless of market prices. For example, buying five hundred dollars of an index fund every month. When prices are high, the fixed sum buys fewer shares; when prices fall, it buys more. Over time the average cost per share sits below the average market price.

The main appeal is behavioral, not mathematical. Most investors cannot time the market, and lump-sum investing at a peak is painful. A fixed schedule removes the decision entirely, which keeps people invested through downturns instead of panic-selling.

Studies comparing lump-sum investing to dollar-cost averaging generally find that investing everything at once wins on average, because markets rise more often than they fall. Dollar-cost averaging wins on regret: it limits the damage of spectacularly bad timing and helps nervous investors participate at all.

It works best with low-cost diversified funds held for years. Applied to a single volatile stock, the same schedule concentrates risk rather than diversifying it. The schedule matters less than the habit: regular contributions, reinvested dividends, and the patience to leave the money alone.
