Factor investing is the idea that stock returns can be explained by exposure to a small set of common characteristics, called factors. Instead of picking individual stocks based on stories, a factor investor systematically tilts a portfolio toward stocks with desirable traits.

The best-known factors come from academic research. The value factor favors stocks that look cheap relative to fundamentals, for example a low price-to-earnings or price-to-book ratio. The momentum factor favors stocks whose prices have been rising over the past 6 to 12 months, on the evidence that trends persist. The quality factor favors profitable companies with stable earnings and low debt. The size factor favors smaller companies, which have historically earned a premium over large caps. The low-volatility factor favors stocks with calmer price movements.

In practice, factor research follows a repeatable loop. First, define the factor precisely, for example "book-to-market ratio in the top quintile." Second, build the signal for every stock in the universe at each point in time. Third, form portfolios that go long the favored stocks and short the opposite end, or simply overweight the favored names. Fourth, backtest the strategy on historical data and check whether the returns survive transaction costs.

A critical discipline is distinguishing real factors from data mining. With thousands of candidate signals, some will look profitable by pure chance. Researchers therefore demand out-of-sample validation, economic intuition for why the factor should work, and persistence across countries and time periods before trusting a new factor.
